THE BRIEF · 1-MINUTE READ

The pitch in one minute

Our belief: learning science should be fun, easy, effortless, convenient, fast and efficient, and students should understand the logic behind every idea instead of memorising it.

Memorised facts fade and break the moment a question changes. Logic you understand lasts: you remember it, you can rebuild it, you can apply it to new problems, and you can create with it.

Built on learning science, not guesswork. Every screen, word and interaction is backed by established principles from cognitive psychology, memory research, motivation science and interface design. Each decision names the principle behind it, and every lesson must pass our checklist before it ships.

Most students experience science as boring lectures, confusing words and pages of repeated working. They memorise formulas to pass, and the understanding fades soon after.

We flip that. Our app feels like a game you play on your phone in a spare ten minutes. Underneath, every tap is designed so the student works out the idea themselves, step by logical step.

Students feel Students gain
Fun: each lesson is a game level that opens with a real-world puzzle. Logic: they predict, test and reason from evidence to a conclusion.
Easy: short plain sentences, one idea per screen, help a tap away. Deep understanding: they discover the rule before they see the equation.
Effortless: no busywork. Effort goes only into the thinking that matters. Lasting memory: they remember because they understand why, and spaced recall keeps it fresh.
Convenient: phone-first, no login, a few minutes at a time, anywhere. Confidence: wrong guesses are part of the game, never a failure.
Fast: a few minutes per level, skipping what they already know. Efficiency: every minute builds understanding, and nothing needs relearning before the exam.

Proof so far. The Lorentz Arcade is a game for HSC magnetism with two playable levels, and a physics tutor uses it before his sessions. Physics comes first, then maths and biology.

Where you come in. You join as a full partner across physics, code and design. Your first big question: how could maths become fun and effortless while making students more logical? (See the last section.)

01Our belief

The belief has two halves. How it feels: fun, easy, effortless, convenient, fast and efficient. What it builds: logical thinking and deep understanding. Each half needs the other. Fun without understanding is just a game, and understanding that feels like a chore never sticks.

"Effortless" means no wasted effort, not no thinking. We remove everything that tires students without teaching them: confusing wording, repeated working, waiting, and fear of being wrong. What's left is the thinking itself, and we make that feel like solving a puzzle rather than doing homework.

02Understand the logic, don't memorise

This is the heart of the app. We never ask a student to memorise something they could work out. When you understand the logic behind an idea, you get four things memorising can't give you.

Memorising gives you Understanding the logic gives you
Understanding A formula you can repeat but can't explain The reason it has to be true
Memory Isolated facts that fade within weeks A connected chain. Forget one link and you can rebuild it from the others.
Application Success only on questions that look like the ones you practised The ability to solve questions you have never seen
Creation Nothing new The ability to design, invent and ask your own questions

The evidence. In Katona's classic experiments (1940), people who learned the principle behind a puzzle remembered it longer and solved new versions better than people who memorised the steps. Ideas processed for meaning are remembered far better than ideas repeated by rote (Craik & Lockhart, 1972). Experts store knowledge as connected principles, not lists of facts (Bransford et al., How People Learn, 2000).

How the app makes logic the default.

  1. Few roots, many results. Each chapter is built on one or two root ideas. Every formula is grown from them in front of the student. For example, r = mv/qB comes from "the magnetic force provides the circular motion", and the lap time comes from "distance ÷ speed". (Schema building, elaboration)
  2. Why before what. Students see what happens and work out why before they meet the formula. The formula becomes a summary of logic they already own. (Concreteness fading)
  3. A visible logic chain. Every reason is built as A → B → C from evidence the student tapped, so they see how ideas connect. (Claim–evidence–reasoning, self-explanation)
  4. Apply to the unseen. After finding a rule, students predict new cases with it before checking. (Transfer)
  5. Create with it. Challenges ask students to design, not repeat, such as tuning a machine that sorts atoms by mass. (Bloom's highest level: create)
  6. Recall means rebuild. Spaced recall asks "why?" and "rebuild it", not "recite it". (Generation effect, retrieval practice)
  7. Even words have logic. Science terms are taught through their roots, so a student who knows that photo means light and synthesis means making can work out what photosynthesis means. This also answers the tutor's point that students can't spell the terms. (Morphological awareness)

Design rules that protect it.

  • No multiple choice while teaching. You predict a free value and answer "why" by tapping evidence. (Generation effect, claim–evidence–reasoning)
  • One reusable routine for finding rules: KNOW → SWAP → CANCEL → CHECK. (Worked-example fading, transfer)
  • Every animation is physically real: taut ropes stay straight, arrows are drawn to scale and timings match the clock. (Perceptual learning)
  • Never ask students to memorise what they can work out. Every formula traces back to a root idea. (Elaboration, schema building)
03False learning vs real learning

Many popular study habits create an illusion of learning. They feel productive because they are smooth and familiar, but little is actually learned. Psychologists call this the illusion of competence: fluency feels like mastery (Bjork, 1994; Kornell & Bjork, 2008). In one review of ten common study techniques, rereading, highlighting and summarising were rated low utility, while self-testing and spaced practice were rated high (Dunlosky et al., 2013).

False learning Why it feels like learning What really happens What our app does instead
Copying notes or a formula a thousand times Your hand gets faster and the page fills up Copying needs no thinking, so the brain stores almost nothing about meaning You build the formula yourself from a root idea, once, and understand it
Rereading and highlighting The text looks more familiar each time Familiarity is mistaken for memory. In the exam, nothing comes back without the page You predict, test and explain, so the idea comes from your own head
Watching a teacher or video solve it Every step makes sense as you watch Following isn't the same as doing. Students who sit through lectures feel they learned more but actually learn less (Deslauriers et al., 2019) Nothing moves until you guess or act
20 near-identical questions in a row You get quicker by question 20 You learn to repeat a pattern, not when to use it, so mixed exam questions break you (Rohrer & Taylor, 2007) A few varied questions, each changing one thing, mixed with older topics
Memorising formulas and answers You can recite them perfectly The facts are isolated, so they fade and can't handle a new question Few root ideas, everything grown from them, so you can rebuild and apply
Cramming the night before You know it the next morning It is gone within weeks Spaced recall after 1 day, 3 days and 1 week
Getting the answer right by guessing from options A tick feels like success You can pick without understanding Free predictions and evidence-based "why", with no options to guess from

Where else today's teaching lets students down. Our tutor told us where his students really get stuck, and research adds the rest.

What students face today Our answer
Technical words stop students from understanding the question (tutor feedback). Real terms in short sentences, with a plain meaning the first time and a tap-to-open reminder after that.
Students write the right equation but get the steps wrong (tutor feedback). You build the reasoning step by step, and the app handles what you already know.
Students can't tell whether an answer makes physical sense (tutor feedback). Before you compute, you predict bigger, same or smaller. Then you check.
Equations come first, so students plug and chug. You discover the rule in the simulation, and the equation arrives last as a shortcut you already understand.
Wrong intuitions survive teaching. Lectures give about half the gain of interactive teaching (Hake, 1998). You commit to a prediction, see the surprise, and work out why.
Being wrong feels like failing, so students stop guessing. Wrong guesses get an orange "Not quite, let's see why" and become the most interesting part of the level.

Real learning can feel harder in the moment, so we make it fun. Predicting, recalling and explaining are "desirable difficulties": they feel slower but build far stronger memory (Bjork). Our job is to make that thinking feel like a game, so students get the real learning without the grind.

04Learn fast and efficiently

Understanding is the fastest route, not a slow one. Memorising feels quick, but you pay for it again and again: you relearn before every test and get stuck on every new kind of question. When you understand the logic once, one root idea gives you many results, and it stays.

Fast means less wasted time, not less thinking. We cut every minute that doesn't build understanding and protect the minutes that do.

Where time is wasted today How our app saves it Principles
Relearning forgotten content before every exam Learn it once with understanding. Reviews arrive just before you would forget, so a 1-minute recall replaces an evening of relearning. Spacing effect, forgetting curve (Ebbinghaus)
Memorising 20 separate formulas Learn 2 root ideas and grow the rest from them Chunking, schema building
Practising what you already know A quick check lets you skip it. Mastered steps fold away, and strong students jump ahead. Mastery learning, expertise reversal
Reading a page to understand one idea A 2-second animation with one line of words Dual coding, multimedia principle (Mayer)
Waiting days for marked homework Instant feedback with the reason Immediate explanatory feedback
Being stuck with no one to ask One targeted nudge at the exact step where your thinking broke Scaffolding, minimal guidance at the point of need
Not knowing what to do next One goal per screen, a clear path and progress pips Goal gradient, cognitive load
Hour-long sessions with fading attention 5–10 minute levels you can fit into a bus ride Microlearning, distributed practice
20 identical practice questions A few varied questions that each teach something new Variation theory, interleaving

We measure speed honestly. For every lesson we track time to mastery and the learning gain per minute, and compare them with a normal lecture plus homework. Our goal is to beat that on both: higher scores in less time.

05Fun

Fun is the engine, not the decoration. Attention is the gateway to memory, and students come back to what they enjoy. Our fun comes from curiosity, choice and the feeling of getting smarter, not from cartoons or points for nothing.

Design rules for fun.

  • Arcade look, with section title cards (PRESS START) and progress pips. (Goal gradient, event segmentation)
  • Plan-first challenges with stars. A ★ needs a first plan that works, so you can't win by wiggling. (Competence, metacognition)
  • A sandbox on the level map that gains new tools with each level cleared. (Autonomy, self-determination theory)

We measure it by whether students finish levels and come back without being told to.

07Effortless

Effortless means all effort goes into thinking. We remove the three things that drain students without teaching them: busywork, friction and fear. The good kind of effort, working out why, stays and feels like solving a puzzle.

Design rules for effortless.

  • One control everywhere: equation tiles you swipe up for more and down for less. The control IS the equation. (Direct manipulation, multiple representations)
  • Response under 100 ms with a haptic tick. (Doherty threshold)
  • Wrong answers are orange "Not quite" with the reason, never red. (Error-friendly framing, psychological safety)

10The psychology that makes it good

Learning science tells us what to teach and in what order. Psychology makes students want to open the app, feel safe in it and keep coming back. We design for the whole student: attention, emotion, motivation, self-belief, habits and memory.

Part of the mind What psychology tells us How we use it in the app
Attention Motion and contrast grab the eye before anything else (preattentive processing). An open question holds attention until it's answered (curiosity gap). Motion and colour are used only for what matters right now. Every level opens with a question and pays it off at the end.
Emotion Anxiety eats working memory, which is why maths anxiety lowers scores (Ashcraft, 2002). Positive emotion widens thinking (Fredrickson, 2001). No red crosses, no countdown timers while learning, anonymous votes and a warm "Not quite, let's see why".
Motivation People are driven by autonomy, competence and relatedness (self-determination theory). Rewards for just doing a task can kill interest in it (the overjustification effect). Stars reward understanding (a plan that works first time), never time spent or taps. Free play gives choice, and debates give connection.
Self-belief The strongest source of self-belief is experiencing success yourself (Bandura's self-efficacy). Treating mistakes as "not yet" keeps students trying (growth mindset). Early levels are designed for quick wins, and difficulty grows step by step. Mistakes are framed as the interesting part.
Memory and surprise Errors made with confidence and then corrected are remembered best (the hypercorrection effect, Butterfield & Metcalfe, 2001). Things that stand out are remembered (von Restorff effect). Students commit to a confident prediction, then see the surprise. That makes the correct idea stick.
Habit A behaviour happens when motivation, ability and a prompt meet (Fogg behaviour model). A 5-minute level is easy to start, and a recall reminder is the prompt.
Endings People judge an experience by its peak and its end (the peak–end rule, Kahneman). Unfinished questions stay on the mind (the Zeigarnik effect). Each level ends on a high: the puzzle answered and a Discovery card. Then one open question carries into the tutorial.
Social learning Arguing with a peer exposes gaps that a teacher's explanation misses (peer instruction). Criticising an idea is easier when it isn't your own. "Who's right?" uses characters, so students judge ideas rather than people. The class votes, then convinces a neighbour.
Perception The eye groups things by colour and closeness (Gestalt principles). We learn from what we see, not from what we're told (perceptual learning). Each symbol shares its colour with its arrow. Every animation is physically real.

What we will never do. We use psychology to help students learn, never to trap their attention. That means no guilt-trip streaks, no loot boxes, no endless feeds, no fake urgency and no other dark patterns. Success is measured in understanding, not screen time.

11How a lesson delivers it

Every lesson follows the same loop, and each step serves one or both halves of the belief.

Step What the student does Feels Builds
1. Puzzle Sees a real-world mystery with an open question Fun Curiosity
2. Predict Sets a guess dial Fun, easy Logic: commit to an idea
3. Play Swipes the equation's letters and watches the sim respond Fun, effortless Intuition from what you see
4. Explain Taps the evidence and builds the reason A → B Easy Logic, deep understanding
5. Equation Grows the formula from the root idea, so it arrives last as a summary of logic they already own Effortless Deep understanding
6. Challenge Uses the logic to design something new, such as tuning a machine that sorts atoms, by planning first and then testing Fun Apply and create
7. Recall Rebuilds the logic after 1 day, 3 days and 1 week, instead of reciting it Convenient Lasting memory

Example: level C3, "How long is one lap?" The level opens with a cyclotron, the machine that makes PET scan medicine. A ball on a chain shows the familiar rule that going faster means a shorter lap. The scene morphs into a magnetic field with the same ball still moving.

The student predicts that doubling the speed will halve the lap time. Then comes the surprise: the lap time stays the same. The student taps the evidence (radius ×2, speed ×2), builds the reason (distance ×2 ÷ speed ×2 = ×1) and finds the rule for themselves. The equation arrives only after they already know why. (Discrepant event, object constancy, concreteness fading)

12Learning together

Arguing an idea with friends is fun, and it is one of the strongest ways to build logic (Mazur; Smith et al., 2009). Two features bring that in.

"Who's right?" cards, in every lesson. Two characters each explain what just happened, and one holds a common mistake. You tap who's right. At the end of the level you discover that one of them was secretly a real scientist, such as Ernest Lawrence. (Contrasting cases, self-explanation, curiosity)

Tutor-session mode, the big feature. The tutor shows a 4-letter code and a QR code, and students join on their phones with no login. The class votes on a prediction and sees the split. Then students get 2 minutes to convince a neighbour, vote again, and run the sim together to see who was right. Votes stay anonymous, so no one fears being wrong. (Peer instruction, psychological safety) This needs a small backend, such as the Firebase or Supabase free tier.

14Your challenge: maths that is fun, effortless and logical

Forget how maths has always been taught, with its rules to memorise and drills to repeat. Today, a student grinds through 20 near-identical questions, substituting and simplifying by hand, and most of that effort teaches nothing new. With AI we can strip out the grind and keep only the thinking.

Luc's starting idea: skip what you already know. A real question mixes several skills. If you have already mastered substitution, you shouldn't have to do it again. Your effort should go only to the part that is new to you.

Starter ideas to react to

Idea How it works Feels Builds
Skip what you know Each question shows as a chain of steps. Steps you have already mastered fold away on their own (one tap to peek), so you only work the new step while still seeing the whole chain. Effortless Logic: you see how the steps connect
Maths you can touch Equations are objects you drag. Move a term across the = and its sign flips; drag a slider and the graph bends. You discover the rules by playing instead of memorising them. Fun, easy Deep understanding of why rules work

Snap your homework Take a photo of a question you're stuck on. The app finds the step where your thinking broke and gives just that one nudge, never the full answer. Convenient, easy Logic: you finish the reasoning yourself
Say it, don't write it Explain your reasoning out loud. The AI follows your chain and asks one question exactly where it breaks. Effortless, convenient Logic, self-explanation
Does it make sense? A quick mini-game before calculating: will the answer be bigger, smaller, positive or negative? Afterwards you check your guess. Fun Number sense, judging answers

What we won't give up

  • The thinking stays with the student. The AI only removes steps you have already proven you can do.
  • Skipped skills are checked now and then, so they don't quietly fade before exams.
  • The maths is always correct. A maths engine does the computing, and the AI only talks.

Where to aim: the hardest topics shared by HSC and HKDSE
